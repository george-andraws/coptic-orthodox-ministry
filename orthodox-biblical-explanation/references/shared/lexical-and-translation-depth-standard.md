# Lexical and Translation Depth Standard for Bible Studies

Use this standard whenever a Bible-study guide includes Greek, Hebrew, Aramaic, Syriac, Septuagint, or translation-shape observations.

## Core principle

Use the internal chain **observed textual fact → supported interpretive consequence → gain to this reader**. Checked with no useful addition is successful editorial work. Do not turn stock Greek synonyms, etymologies or verbal resemblance into an obligation to add a note or proof of authorial intent. The John 6 subsection below is book-specific precedent: load it for that passage or a directly relevant comparison, not every guide.

A language note is valuable only when it changes how the reader understands the passage. It should reveal movement in the text, sharpen theology, protect against a shallow or false reading, connect Scripture to worship or the Fathers, or give the servant a concrete teaching handle.

Do not treat “linguistic insight” as a generic explanation of an English phrase. A sentence like “He who sent me places John's ministry under divine command” may be true, but it is not a language insight unless the wording, original term, translation choice, or source-text shape reveals something the English reader would likely miss.

Do not add vocabulary trivia. A note like “the Greek word means love” or “the Hebrew word means see” is usually not enough. The note must answer: **What does this wording make visible that the English reader would likely miss?**

For New Testament guides, actively look for meaningful original-language insight when the passage warrants it. Greek and occasional Aramaic terms can carry theological weight that English flattens: λόγος / Logos as “Word” in John, παράκλητος / Paraklētos behind “Helper” or “Comforter” for the Holy Spirit, or distinct Greek love words such as ἀγάπη / agapē, ἀγαπάω / agapaō, φιλέω / phileō, φιλαδελφία / philadelphia, φιλοξενία / philoxenia, and disordered `phil-` compounds when English flattens them as “love,” “beloved,” “brotherly love,” “hospitality,” or “lovers of...” Include these only when they deepen interpretation, Christology, pneumatology, sacramental meaning, worship, pastoral guardrails, or spiritual application.

## What excellent lexical depth does

Good language work usually does one or more of these:

1. **Shows movement or escalation in the passage.** Example: in John 6, English “eat” hides the movement from ordinary ἐσθίω / φάγω eating language to the more concrete τρώγω after the hearers object to Christ's flesh.
2. **Distinguishes similar English renderings.** If one English word translates several Greek or Hebrew terms, explain the difference and why the author chose or shifted terms. This matters especially in NT passages where English “love,” “word,” “helper,” “comforter,” “repent,” “witness,” “believe,” or “abide” may hide a more precise Greek term, a deliberate repetition, or a theological title.
3. **Unpacks key theological terms whose English gloss is too thin.** Explain the original term when the word itself carries doctrinal or spiritual weight, such as Logos for Christ, Paraklētos for the Holy Spirit, or agapaō / phileō distinctions when the authorial choice affects the passage. Do not force a distinction where context or scholarship does not support it.
4. **Protects doctrine and worship.** Use wording details to guard against symbol-only, crude, moralistic, anti-incarnational, anti-sacramental, anti-Trinitarian, anti-Jewish, or other distorted readings when the text itself warrants it.
5. **Explains source-shape or numbering differences.** LXX / MT / NKJV / Brenton differences matter when they change verse order, emphasis, patristic interpretation, lectionary matching, or the reader's ability to follow the text.
6. **Reveals biblical intertextuality.** Show echoes, repeated roots, wordplay, wilderness or Exodus language, sacrificial vocabulary, covenant terms, or Johannine / Pauline repeated vocabulary when it affects meaning.
7. **Supports patristic and liturgical reading.** If a Father, hymn, lectionary placement, or icon depends on a word or translation shape, explain the word where it appears in the passage.
8. **Deepens spiritual application.** The note should lead somewhere: repentance, prayer, Eucharistic reverence, watchfulness, humility, hope, mercy, or communion with Christ.
9. **Names and places.** When a Hebrew or Greek name or place has theological payoff, give the sense and why it matters here: Nathanael, Cephas, Nazareth / *netzer*, Cana, Galilee, Bethsaida. Do not add onomastic trivia.

## Required handling

For each language insight:

1. **Quote the exact word or phrase being discussed in context.** Identify the verse or line. Use quotation marks for mentioned English terms in headings and prose, including each item of a list: `Language insight: “witness”, “right”, and “children of God”`. This distinguishes words under discussion from ordinary prose. Do not alter the text or punctuation of an approved Scripture quotation.
2. **Give the original term when useful.** Use Greek/Hebrew/Aramaic script when possible, and transliteration if it helps a normal reader. Distinguish the verse's actual inflected form from the dictionary lemma: identify which you are presenting, and do not imply that the headword itself is the printed verse form. Verify both against the named textual witness and lexical entry, because a correct root does not authenticate an incorrectly attributed form.
3. **Establish the translation’s source reading, then translate plainly.** Before saying an English edition “renders” a Greek word, compare that edition’s underlying reading with the named Greek witness actually opened. Classify a difference as a translation choice, textual variant, or unresolved source question; a critical-text interlinear cannot establish which word the NKJV translates. Define the attested term in normal English without pretending the lexicon entry itself settles the whole interpretation. Reserve claims about manuscript age or majority support for an inspected apparatus or documented textual source, because a parallel-editions page establishes edition differences, not their manuscript distribution.
4. **Explain why it matters from the displayed Scripture.** Tie the observation to the passage’s argument, Christology, pneumatology, sacramental reality, pastoral guardrail, worship, or spiritual fruit. Check subject, person, number, and antecedent against the approved English block before deriving an application. If another textual witness changes “we” to “you,” explain that difference explicitly rather than silently interpreting its pronoun beneath the displayed edition. When multiple antecedents fit the grammar, present the live alternatives and use context to weigh them; proximity alone does not settle a pronoun whose gender and number fit both.
5. **Show the reader's gain.** State what the original wording, repeated root, term choice, or translation shape lets the reader see that a generic English explanation would miss.
6. **Add direct source URLs in source notes when online lexical/interlinear sources are used.** Do not cite vague homepages. For a claimed lexical explanation, read the relevant numbered sense and its cited verse examples, not merely the entry's first gloss; preserve the exact sense locator privately. Keep an attested meaning, a proposed translation mechanism and an inferred intertextual echo separate, because a lexicon can support the first without establishing the other two. Before writing “only,” “once more,” or another occurrence count, check the complete claimed corpus by lemma, including inflected forms; otherwise use the supported “also.” For a proposed verbal echo or name-pun in another author, inspect that author's recurring usage before claiming intentional dependence, because habitual phrasing can explain a genuine resemblance. Verify the chronology independently before making a repeated word evidence that one biblical person had already enacted the appeal; canonical book order does not establish event order.
7. **Check extraction completeness before comparing or quoting Scripture.** Confirm that the retrieved text actually contains the requested verse and its surrounding context; an HTTP success or a page titled with the right chapter is insufficient. Brenton chapter extractions can return only opening verses and an ellipsis even when the original HTML contains the whole chapter. If the verse is missing or the extraction contains a truncation marker, fetch the same primary URL directly, preserve the response, and parse its text with an HTML parser. Resolve verse labels and separate cross-reference or footnote text before copying the quotation; a flattened page can insert editorial apparatus into the verse. Keep the full witness privately and return only located excerpts to the working context. Do not interpret extraction loss as absence from the biblical edition.

## Guardrails

- Treat a successful web extraction as provisional when a chapter contains literal ellipses or far fewer verses than expected. Some Brenton eBible pages yield only their opening verses through clean-text extraction even though the fetched HTML contains the whole chapter. Retrieve and preserve that same page's full HTML with a standard HTTP client, extract the actual verse spans, and verify the exact words and printed labels before quoting. An HTTP 200 or a correct chapter title does not establish passage coverage.
- When mapping Septuagint and Hebrew chapter sequences, map actual contents rather than applying a verse-offset formula. Name the adopted edition: Brenton, Swete/Cambridge, and CCAT-derived Greek can subdivide the same passage differently. Verify both internal numbering gaps and independently established chapter endings; a shorter chapter is not evidence that its final verses were dropped. A remapped website's `OMITTED TEXT` label may conceal material present elsewhere, so check the actual words before declaring content absent. Preserve approved canonical Scripture byte differences instead of silently normalizing them to an extractor or comparator.
- Name the actual Greek witness when explaining a translation difficulty, and compare its words rather than treating “the Greek” as uniform. Compare the relevant editions at each verse; agreement between the Textus Receptus and Majority Text at one variant does not establish agreement at another. Identify a named ecclesial edition by its actual title and date rather than presenting it as the universal Greek text of every Orthodox Church. Electronic Swete and CCAT-derived texts can differ even within the same chapter and verse; one comparator cannot authorize silently correcting the approved Scripture. Check earlier commands in the same adopted translation too: a familiar Hebrew-based construction description may differ from Brenton both in the command and its execution.
- For guides with NKJV-first headings and retained Brenton verse labels, keep an ordered internal source-verse plan that records each chunk’s own source chapter and exact printed labels; do not derive source lookup coordinates from the primary heading. Compare source words and clause divisions before mapping a skipped label or a reordered law. A dual-reference heading can make a legacy verifier select the final Septuagint parenthesis as the primary reference: inspect the actual diagnostic and validate NKJV Teaching Notes coverage separately rather than altering correct citations to satisfy that parser.
- Do not overclaim. A lexical observation can support an interpretation, but a single word rarely proves the whole doctrine by itself.
- Do not flatten later or broader usage. If a word can mean both a vivid concrete action and ordinary use in later Greek, say so.
- Do not use language notes to show off. If the note does not help a parish servant teach the passage better, omit it.
- Do not collect all language notes into a late appendix in guide bodies. Put each one under the passage section it illuminates.
- Do not let language notes replace exegesis. The note should serve the text's movement, not become a detached mini-lecture.

## Model example: John 6 eating verbs

Strong pattern:

> John's repeated English “eat” hides a Greek progression. Through verse 53, the forms are tied to φάγω, ordinary eating or consuming language that supplies certain tenses for ἐσθίω. In verses 54, 56, 57, and 58, John uses τρώγω: to gnaw or chew, and more generally to eat. The shift should not be exaggerated into a claim that τρώγω can only mean animal-like gnawing in every context. But John places it exactly where the dispute over Christ's flesh becomes sharp. The language becomes more concrete and sustained.
>
> After the question, “How can this Man give us His flesh to eat?” Jesus does not retreat into a safer metaphor. In verse 58, the fathers “ate” (ἔφαγον) manna and died, but the one “eating / feeding on” (ὁ τρώγων) this bread lives forever. The English word “eat” can miss the contrast. John presses the reader from ordinary eating, to the manna type, to real communion with the life-giving flesh of the incarnate Word.

Why this works:

- It distinguishes multiple Greek terms hidden behind one English word.
- It explains textual movement, not isolated vocabulary.
- It strengthens Eucharistic realism while avoiding crude or exaggerated claims.
- It ties the lexical note to Incarnation, manna typology, resurrection, and communion.
- It gives a servant something concrete to teach.

Weak pattern:

> The Greek word for eat is τρώγω, which means gnaw. Therefore John 6 proves the Eucharist is literal.

Why this fails:

- It overclaims.
- It ignores broader usage.
- It makes the word do the whole theological argument alone.
- It risks crude imagination instead of spiritual, incarnational, Eucharistic reading.
