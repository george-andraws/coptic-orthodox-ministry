# The Holy Appetite

## Hunger and thirst for righteousness

> “Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness,
> For they shall be filled.”
> Matthew 5:6

## Main burden

Every person is hungry. The question is not whether we desire deeply, but what has been given the right to command our desire. Christ does not ask us to become people without appetite. He heals appetite. He turns the intensity we give to comfort, approval, money, control, distraction, or being right toward righteousness: a life made right with God, truthful before Him, merciful toward our neighbor, and ready to do the Father's will. The promise is not that we will fill ourselves. It is that those who hunger for what God loves will be filled by God Himself.

## Opening: what do you reach for when you are empty?

There is a moment between the pressure and the choice.

A hard conversation ends. A child disappoints you. You feel invisible at work. You see someone else receiving what you wanted. You are tired, lonely, angry, or afraid.

What do you reach for?

For some of us, it is food or a screen. For others, it is an argument we replay until we win it. It may be shopping, work, gossip, resentment, pornography, a drink, a fantasy of escape, or the quiet demand to be noticed and thanked. None of these cravings arrives by accident. They promise to relieve the ache.

Christ looks at people who know hunger and thirst, then says:

> “Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be filled.”

He does not say, “Blessed are those who have no desires.” He says, “Bring your desire to its true object.”

The question for this week is not merely, **“What bad habit should I stop?”** It is:

**What do my time, attention, imagination, and strongest reactions reveal that I actually hunger for?**

## 1. The hunger beneath the hunger

The fourth Beatitude follows a holy sequence. Poverty of spirit puts down the illusion that we are self-sufficient. Mourning tells the truth about sin and sorrow. Meekness puts strength under the rule of God. Then Christ reaches the deepest level: desire itself.

The word is not mild. Christ speaks of those who **hunger** and **thirst**. These are not passing preferences. They are the body’s cry for what it cannot live without.

The world trains us to treat desire as authority. If I want it, I deserve it. If it feels authentic, it must be good. If another person blocks it, they have become my enemy.

But desire is not a reliable god. It can be wounded, trained badly, and captured by the passions. A desire can be real without being trustworthy. A craving can be intense without telling us the truth about what will give life.

James exposes the issue with uncomfortable clarity in this Sunday’s Catholic Epistle: “Where do wars and fights come from among you? Do they not come from your desires for pleasure that war in your members?” (James 4:1). The conflict outside us often begins with an appetite inside us that has become sovereign.

This is why fasting matters. Fasting is not contempt for food, the body, or celebration. It is a small and truthful refusal to let every desire become a command. When the body says, “Now,” the Christian learns to answer, “Not every desire gets to lead me.” Fasting makes space for a larger hunger.

## 2. What does “righteousness” mean?

If righteousness means only private rule-keeping, this Beatitude will make us hard. If it means only public concern for injustice, it will make us angry at the world while leaving our own hearts untouched.

In Scripture, righteousness is larger.

It includes uprightness before God, faithfulness to His will, mercy toward the neighbor, justice in how we use power and money, truthfulness in speech, purity in the hidden heart, and the restoration of right relationship. It is not a polished religious image. It is life brought into alignment with God.

That is why Isaiah 58 belongs beside Matthew 5:6. God rejects a fast that leaves the worshiper oppressive, self-absorbed, and indifferent to suffering. The fast He chooses looses the bonds of wickedness, shares bread with the hungry, shelters the poor, and refuses to hide from one’s own flesh (Isaiah 58:6–7).

Righteousness therefore includes how a husband speaks to his wife when no one is listening. It includes what a parent does with irritation. It includes whether we pay fairly, tell the truth when it costs us, notice the person whom nobody notices, and stop using someone else’s weakness as material for our own superiority.

But it is still more than ethics. We hunger for righteousness because we hunger for God. We want His will to become dearer to us than our own self-protection.

Saint John Chrysostom sees both dimensions. He says that righteousness here can mean the whole of virtue, and it also confronts covetousness directly. Christ names hunger and thirst because He wants us to pursue righteousness “not merely anyhow, but with all desire.” He takes the intensity usually spent on gaining more and redirects it toward freedom from acquisition’s grip.[^chrysostom]

This is not less than justice. It is the only place justice can become clean. A person who longs for righteousness will not use mercy as a performance, or advocacy as a way to feel morally superior. He wants what God wants, beginning with the heart God can actually heal.

## 3. Christ has food we do not see

The Beatitude is first fulfilled in Christ.

At the well in Samaria, after His disciples return with food, Jesus says:

> “I have food to eat of which you do not know. … My food is to do the will of Him who sent Me, and to finish His work.”
> John 4:32, 34

He has just met a woman exhausted by false wells: relationships that could not heal her thirst, a community that had marked her by her past, a religion marked by division. Christ does not flatter her. He tells the truth. Yet He offers living water, reveals Himself, and sends her back into the town as a witness.

Then He names His own food: the Father’s will and the work of salvation.

This is not a rejection of ordinary bodily needs. Christ eats, drinks, accepts hospitality, and feeds crowds. It is a revelation of what carries Him at the deepest level. The Son does not live from self-will. His life is communion with the Father, poured out for the world.

Here is the difference between a religious appetite and Christ’s holy appetite. We can hunger to be admired for doing good. Christ hungers to do the Father’s will, even when it leads to the Cross. We can thirst to correct people. Christ thirsts to save them. We can want justice mainly because someone has injured us. Christ brings justice by taking the injury of the world into His own flesh and breaking its power through His Resurrection.

If the Beatitude becomes only a command, it will crush us. But Christ does not merely tell us to hunger. He gives us His own life. In Baptism we are joined to Him. In the Eucharist He feeds us with Himself. In confession He exposes the false foods by which we have tried to live and restores us to communion.

The One who says, “They shall be filled,” is not pointing to a distant reward alone. He is the Bread of Life. He is the Living Water. He is already giving Himself to His people.

## 4. The false fullness that leaves us empty

The counterfeits of this Beatitude are not always dramatic. Often they look ordinary.

### Comfort without communion

Rest is good. Food is good. Entertainment can be good. But when comfort becomes our highest good, every interruption begins to feel like an injustice. We become hard to live with because we have made convenience sacred.

### Being right without becoming righteous

Some people hunger for the correct answer, the winning argument, the better take. But righteousness is not the pleasure of exposing another person. Truth belongs to righteousness, but truth without mercy can become a weapon in the hands of pride.

### Success without faithfulness

Achievement can become a false food. We tell ourselves we are providing, serving, building, or leading. Then we discover that we cannot rest, cannot pray, cannot receive correction, and cannot be present with our family because the next accomplishment has become our bread.

### Justice without repentance

The hunger for justice is holy. Scripture commands it. But if we can name every injustice except our own manipulation, contempt, dishonesty, and self-indulgence, we are not yet hungry for righteousness. We are hungry to be innocent without being changed.

James gives this warning to the wealthy who have accumulated at the expense of others: “You have lived on the earth in pleasure and luxury” while wages were withheld and the vulnerable were harmed (James 5:1–5). This is not a warning reserved for rich people in an ancient text. It asks every one of us: whose labor, patience, attention, or peace am I quietly consuming in order to stay comfortable?

Luke’s corresponding woe should stay beside us: “Woe to you who are full, for you shall hunger” (Luke 6:25). The danger is not fullness as a gift. The danger is becoming so full of lesser things that we no longer notice our need for God.

## 5. Isaiah’s fast and the appetite of mercy

Isaiah 58 refuses the lie that spiritual life can be separated from the neighbor’s suffering.

God asks:

> “Is this not the fast that I have chosen:
> To loose the bonds of wickedness,
> To undo the heavy burdens,
> To let the oppressed go free,
> And that you break every yoke?”
> Isaiah 58:6

Then the prophet names bread, shelter, clothing, and the refusal to hide from one’s own flesh.

The passage does not say: replace prayer with social action. It says that prayer, fasting, worship, money, and mercy belong to one life. A fast that makes us more irritable, more judgmental, and less generous has become a diet with religious vocabulary.

The person who hungers for righteousness notices the needs near him. He does not only care about suffering that can become a public statement. He sees the exhausted spouse, the overwhelmed single parent, the lonely elderly person, the new family at church, the coworker being quietly excluded, the neighbor who cannot repay a favor.

This is where the Beatitude becomes costly. We may want righteousness in theory while protecting the very habits that prevent mercy in practice: the budget with no room for generosity, the schedule with no room for people, the conversation in which we will not apologize, the resentment we keep because it has become part of our identity.

Christ does not fill this hunger by making us feel noble. He fills it by making us merciful.

## 6. How the Church retrains desire

The Church does not simply tell us to want better things. She gives us a new pattern of desire.

We fast so that hunger can become prayer rather than panic. We give alms so that money stops pretending to be security. We confess so that hidden desires lose their right to rule in the dark. We pray the Psalms so that David’s thirst becomes our own:

> “As the deer pants for the water brooks,
> So pants my soul for You, O God.
> My soul thirsts for God, for the living God.”
> Psalm 42:1–2 (LXX Psalm 41:1–2)

The Church’s Scripture and worship do not deny the body. They teach the body whom it belongs to.

The Eucharist is the center. At the altar, we do not bring God our self-satisfied fullness. We come hungry. We receive not a symbol of our own goodness, but the Body and Blood of the Lord who gave Himself for the life of the world. This is why the promise “they shall be filled” cannot be separated from the Church. The filling is communion.

We should be careful here. No one should turn fasting into a measure of spiritual worth or a weapon against people with medical, psychological, or economic burdens. The purpose is not to prove that we are strong. It is to learn that God is good enough to be desired above what usually controls us.

## A practice for this week: name the appetite, offer the appetite

For seven days, take five quiet minutes before bed and ask:

1. **What did I reach for today when I felt empty?**
2. **What did I think that thing would give me?**
3. **What righteous desire was underneath the counterfeit?** Perhaps rest, comfort, security, beauty, belonging, peace, or joy.
4. **How can I bring that true desire to Christ tomorrow?**

Then pray simply:

> Lord Jesus Christ, You are the Bread of Life and the Living Water. Show me what I am feeding, and make me hungry for what is righteous. Free me from every appetite that makes me smaller. Fill me with love for Your will and mercy for my neighbor. Amen.

Choose one concrete act as well:

- Fast from one nonessential comfort for a day, then give the saved money or time to someone else.
- Repair one small injustice: pay what is owed, apologize without explanation, return a call you have been avoiding, or speak up for someone being diminished.
- Read Isaiah 58:6–10 slowly before your next meal.

## Personal contemplation

Imagine Christ at Jacob’s well.

He is tired from the journey. He is thirsty. Yet He is not ruled by exhaustion. A wounded woman comes near, and He has room for her. He does not use her need. He meets it.

Then hear Him say, “My food is to do the will of Him who sent Me.”

What has been feeding on you lately? What appetite has taken more from you than it has given?

Do not begin with shame. Bring the hunger itself to Christ.

He does not despise a thirsty soul. He is already at the well.

*[Pause for 15–20 seconds.]*

## Conclusion

The blessed life is not desirelessness. It is desire healed.

Christ does not merely take away the false foods. He gives a hunger large enough to make us free: a hunger for righteousness, for the Father’s will, for mercy, truth, purity, and the healing of the neighbor.

The world says, “Get enough so that you never have to need.” Christ says, “Come to Me hungry.”

The world says, “Take what you can.” Christ says, “Hunger for what is right.”

The world says, “Protect your comfort.” Christ says, “Give yourself in love.”

And He does not leave us empty. He gives Himself as bread. He gives His Spirit as living water. He turns the emptiness we fear into the place where communion begins.

## Closing prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, You hungered to do the will of the Father and thirsted for the salvation of the world. Forgive us for the lesser things we have made into bread. Free us from desire that becomes possession, comfort that becomes indifference, and justice that refuses repentance. Give us a holy appetite: to love what You love, seek what is right, serve the person before us, and receive You with open hands. Fill us with Your life in the mysteries of Your Church, and make our hunger a path to mercy. Amen.

## Teaching guide

### 30-minute teaching plan

| Time | Section | Aim |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–4:00 | Opening: what do you reach for when empty? | Let people recognize real hunger without shaming them. |
| 4:00–8:00 | The hunger beneath the hunger | Connect to the first four Beatitudes and introduce desire as a spiritual issue. |
| 8:00–13:00 | What righteousness means | Refuse the false choice between private holiness and justice for the neighbor. |
| 13:00–18:00 | Christ’s food at the well | Make John 4 the Christological center. |
| 18:00–22:00 | False fullness | Name comfort, being right, achievement, and selective justice plainly. |
| 22:00–26:00 | Isaiah’s fast and Church practice | Move from fasting to mercy, confession, almsgiving, and Eucharist. |
| 26:00–30:00 | Weekly practice, contemplation, and prayer | Leave the room at the well with Christ, not merely with self-monitoring. |

- **Ask:** Use one or two questions that let people identify a false appetite without requiring disclosure of a private struggle.
- **Emphasize:** Christ heals desire rather than merely suppressing it; the promise of fullness is communion with Him.
- **Watch for:** Do not weaponize fasting or reduce righteousness to either private rule-keeping or activism without repentance.
- **Connect:** Let John 4, Isaiah 58, James 4–5, and the Eucharist carry one coherent movement from desire to mercy and communion.

### Ask

- What do you most instinctively reach for when you feel empty, anxious, ashamed, or unseen?
- What is the difference between wanting to be right and hungering for righteousness?
- Where has comfort become a demand that makes you less available to God or people?
- What does Isaiah 58 expose about a fast, prayer, or religious practice that leaves us unchanged toward the neighbor?
- What righteous desire may be hidden beneath one of your recurring false appetites?

### Emphasize

- Christ does not call us to become people without desire. He heals and redirects desire.
- Righteousness includes holiness, justice, faithfulness, mercy, truth, and right relationship with God and neighbor.
- Hunger for righteousness is not a way to earn God. It is the response of a life being formed by Christ.
- John 4 is the center: Christ’s food is the Father’s will and the salvation of the wounded person before Him.
- Isaiah 58 holds fasting and mercy together. Private devotion that protects indifference is not the fast God chooses.
- “They shall be filled” reaches its center in communion with Christ, especially in the Eucharistic life of the Church.

### Watch for

- Do not reduce righteousness to political activism or to private rule-keeping. The Beatitude confronts both a piety without mercy and activism without repentance.
- Do not use fasting language to shame people with eating disorders, illness, pregnancy, demanding labor, financial hardship, or other real constraints. Pastoral fasting must be guided by a father of confession.
- Do not promise that faithful people will always receive material comfort, career success, or visible justice in this life.
- Do not imply that every desire is sinful. The issue is not desire itself but desire severed from God and allowed to rule us.
- Do not claim Matthew 5:6 is the appointed Sunday Gospel. The confirmed Sunday readings include James 4:7–5:5, which supplies a real thematic connection through its warning about disordered pleasure and unjust wealth.

### Connect

- Connect poverty of spirit to the end of self-sufficiency; mourning to sorrow over what sin has done; meekness to strength under God; then hunger to desire re-ordered toward God.
- Connect James 4 to the fights that begin when pleasure becomes sovereign.
- Connect John 4 to Christ’s own holy appetite: not self-display, but the Father’s will and a wounded woman’s salvation.
- Connect Isaiah 58 to concrete acts of mercy, fair dealing, and hospitality.
- Connect Psalm 42 to the Church’s language of thirst for the living God.
- Connect the Eucharist to the promise of being filled: Christ Himself is not a reward after the lesson but the food of the Church now.

### Show

No single canonical icon is central enough to carry this lesson without forcing the visual connection. If building slides, use a respectful, clearly labeled public-domain or licensed icon of Christ and the Samaritan Woman at the well only after verifying provenance. Show it during John 4, not as decorative background. The visual purpose is the contrast between the woman’s false wells and Christ’s living water, and then Christ’s declaration that His food is the Father’s will. Do not use a generated image as if it were a canonical icon.

## Iconography connection

The meeting of Christ and the Samaritan Woman at the well is the most meaningful iconographic scene for this lesson. It places literal thirst, wounded desire, truth-telling, living water, and mission in one Gospel encounter. The Samaritan Woman comes for ordinary water and finds the One who knows her life without humiliating her. Christ reveals Himself and transforms her from someone avoiding her community into a witness who calls the town to Him.

A verified public-domain or clearly licensed image was not located in the lesson-library search at drafting time, so this lesson does not embed an image with uncertain rights. If an icon is later selected for a deck, use it while teaching John 4 rather than treating it as generic religious art.

## Glossary

- **Beatitude:** Christ’s declaration of who is blessed in His Kingdom and the promise belonging to that life.
- **Righteousness:** Life made right before God and expressed in faithfulness, holiness, mercy, justice, truth, and love of neighbor. It is more than private morality and more than public concern.
- **Passions:** Disordered movements of desire that seek a created thing as if it were God, such as greed, vainglory, anger, lust, envy, or love of comfort.
- **Covetousness:** The grasping desire to acquire, possess, or secure oneself through more. Chrysostom names it as a direct counterpoint to hunger for righteousness.
- **Asceticism:** The Church’s disciplined training of desire through fasting, prayer, almsgiving, watchfulness, and repentance. It is medicine for the soul, not a performance.
- **Eucharist:** The Mystery in which the Church receives the true Body and Blood of Christ, the food of eternal life and communion with Him.
