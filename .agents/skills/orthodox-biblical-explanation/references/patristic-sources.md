# Patristic Reference Guide

This file provides a working reference of the Church Fathers most commonly drawn upon when explaining Scripture from an Orthodox perspective. It is meant to help the skill stay grounded in real positions and avoid fabricating quotations or attributions. When uncertain about a Father's specific position, do not invent a citation. Use "the patristic tradition holds" only when a real broad tradition is known; otherwise say that no verified source has been found yet or paraphrase without naming a Father.

## The Alexandrian School

The Alexandrian tradition emphasizes the spiritual, allegorical, and Christological reading of Scripture. It sees every text as opening onto Christ and the mysteries.

**St. Athanasius the Great (c. 296 to 373)**
Patriarch of Alexandria. Defender of the Nicene faith against Arianism. His writings central to Christology, especially "On the Incarnation." Reads Old Testament heavily through the lens of the Word made flesh. Particularly strong on Psalms (his Letter to Marcellinus on the Psalms).

**St. Cyril of Alexandria (c. 376 to 444)**
Patriarch of Alexandria. Champion of the Theotokos title at Ephesus (431). Massive exegetical output: commentaries on John, Luke, the Pentateuch, Isaiah, the Twelve Prophets. Strong typological reader. Sees the Old Testament saturated with Christ.

**Origen (c. 185 to 254)**
The most prolific exegete of the early Church. Founder of systematic allegorical reading. To be cited with care: some of his speculative views on apokatastasis (universal restoration) and pre-existence of souls were condemned at the Fifth Ecumenical Council. His exegetical method, however, profoundly shaped Orthodox interpretation. When citing Origen, draw from his exegetical works (homilies on Genesis, Exodus, Numbers, Joshua, Song of Songs) rather than his speculative theology.

**Didymus the Blind (c. 313 to 398)**
Successor to Origen at the Catechetical School of Alexandria. Blind from age four. Commentaries on Genesis, Job, Zechariah, Psalms. Subject to caution due to association with Origenist controversies, but his exegetical insights are widely respected.

**Clement of Alexandria (c. 150 to 215)**
Predecessor of Origen. "Stromata," "Paedagogus," "Protrepticus." More philosophical than exegetical, but useful for the integration of faith and Hellenic thought.

## The Antiochene School

The Antiochene tradition emphasizes the literal, historical, and grammatical reading of Scripture, while still affirming typology. It is suspicious of allegory that floats free of the text.

**St. John Chrysostom (c. 347 to 407)**
Archbishop of Constantinople. The "Golden Mouth." The greatest preacher of the early Church. Homilies on Matthew, John, Acts, all of Paul's letters, Genesis, the Psalms, Isaiah. His exegesis is moral, pastoral, vivid. Often the first Father to consult for any New Testament passage. Strong on the practical and the moral; equally strong on Christology when the text demands it.

**Theodoret of Cyrus (c. 393 to 458)**
Bishop, exegete, historian. Commentaries on most of the Old Testament and the Pauline epistles. Known for clarity and balance. Holds the Antiochene literal sense while allowing typology where Scripture itself supports it.

**Theodore of Mopsuestia (c. 350 to 428)**
Important Antiochene exegete. Some of his Christological views were condemned at the Fifth Ecumenical Council, particularly those associated with Nestorian tendencies. Cite with care; his exegetical method is influential but his Christology requires caution.

**Diodore of Tarsus (d. c. 390)**
Teacher of both Chrysostom and Theodore. Founder of the Antiochene school's mature method. Most works lost.

## The Cappadocian Fathers

These three giants shaped the Church's articulation of the Trinity and the Christian life.

**St. Basil the Great (c. 330 to 379)**
Archbishop of Caesarea. "Hexaemeron" (homilies on the six days of creation), homilies on the Psalms, ascetic works. Father of Eastern monasticism through his rules.

**St. Gregory of Nazianzus, the Theologian (c. 329 to 390)**
Archbishop of Constantinople briefly. Theological orations. One of only three figures in Orthodox tradition called "the Theologian" (with John the Evangelist and Symeon the New Theologian). His sermons often weave Scripture into densely theological reflection.

**St. Gregory of Nyssa (c. 335 to 395)**
Bishop of Nyssa. "Life of Moses," "Commentary on the Song of Songs," "On the Making of Man." Profoundly mystical reader of Scripture. Sees the spiritual life as endless ascent (epektasis).

## The Syriac Fathers

The Syriac tradition reads Scripture poetically and typologically, often with extraordinary tenderness.

**St. Ephrem the Syrian (c. 306 to 373)**
The Harp of the Spirit. Hymns, commentaries on Genesis, Exodus, the Diatessaron. His readings are densely typological, deeply Marian, profoundly Christological. The Coptic and Syriac traditions both venerate him highly.

**St. Isaac the Syrian / Isaac of Nineveh (7th century)**
Ascetic and mystical writer. "Ascetical Homilies." Less an exegete than a teacher of the inner life, but his quotations of Scripture are luminous. Drawn upon heavily for the spiritual application of any passage.

**Aphrahat the Persian Sage (c. 270 to 345)**
"Demonstrations." Pre-Nicene Syriac theologian. Important for typology, especially of the Cross.

## The Desert and Ascetic Tradition

These Fathers read Scripture from inside the cell, the desert, and the practice of unceasing prayer.

**St. Macarius the Great (c. 300 to 391)**
Egyptian desert father. Homilies attributed to him (the Macarian Homilies, with debated authorship) profoundly shaped Eastern spirituality. Reads Scripture as describing the inner journey of the soul.

**Evagrius Ponticus (c. 345 to 399)**
Disciple of the Cappadocians, monk in the Egyptian desert. Important for ascetic theology and the eight passions (later refined into seven by John Cassian). Cite with care: condemned for some Origenist positions, but his teaching on watchfulness and the passions is integrated into the Philokalia.

**St. John Cassian (c. 360 to 435)**
Bridge between East and West. "Conferences" and "Institutes." Key for the four senses of Scripture (literal, allegorical, tropological, anagogical) in the Latin tradition. Integrates desert wisdom into a transmissible form.

**St. John of the Ladder / Climacus (c. 579 to 649)**
Abbot of Sinai. "The Ladder of Divine Ascent." Read aloud during Lent in monasteries. His treatment of the passions and virtues is densely scriptural.

**St. Maximus the Confessor (c. 580 to 662)**
One of the deepest Christological and exegetical minds of the Church. "Ambigua," "Questions to Thalassius," "Mystagogia." Sees Christ as the key to all reality and all Scripture. The natural reference for cosmic and Christological readings.

## The Latin Fathers

Recognized in both Eastern and Oriental Orthodox traditions, though with some distinctions in reception.

**St. Jerome (c. 347 to 420)**
Translator of the Vulgate. Massive exegetical output: commentaries on most of the prophets, the Psalms, the Pauline epistles, and others. Bridge between Hebrew scholarship and the Christian tradition. Particularly valuable when Hebrew nuance illuminates a passage.

**St. Augustine of Hippo (c. 354 to 430)**
Bishop of Hippo. "Confessions," "City of God," "On Christian Doctrine," extensive sermons and tractates on Scripture. Deeply formative for the West; received in the East with respect but with some reservations on certain points (predestination, original sin's transmission, the filioque debate is later). For Scripture specifically, his "Tractates on the Gospel of John" and "Expositions on the Psalms" are widely valued in Orthodox use.

**St. Ambrose of Milan (c. 339 to 397)**
Teacher of Augustine. Hymns, homilies, "On the Mysteries," "On the Sacraments." Strong typological reader, especially of the Old Testament.

**St. Gregory the Great (c. 540 to 604)**
Pope of Rome. "Moralia in Job," "Pastoral Rule," "Dialogues." His reading of Job as a vast moral and Christological allegory is foundational. Often called St. Gregory the Dialogist in Eastern Orthodox usage.

**St. Leo the Great (c. 400 to 461)**
Pope of Rome. His Tome formative for Chalcedon's Christology, accepted in Eastern Orthodoxy but rejected by the Oriental Orthodox tradition (including Coptic). When citing Leo, be aware of the Christological history; cite his exegetical material rather than his Christological formulations when working with a Coptic Orthodox audience.

## Later Byzantine Fathers

**St. Symeon the New Theologian (c. 949 to 1022)**
The third figure called "the Theologian." Mystical writer on the experience of the divine light. Hymns of Divine Love, Catechetical Discourses. Reads Scripture from within the experience of God.

**St. Gregory Palamas (c. 1296 to 1359)**
Archbishop of Thessalonica. Defender of hesychasm. The distinction between the divine essence and energies. His scriptural reading is integrated with his theological vision of theosis.

**St. Nicholas Cabasilas (c. 1322 to 1392)**
"The Life in Christ," "Commentary on the Divine Liturgy." Profound exegete of the sacramental dimension of Scripture.

## Coptic Orthodox Specific Considerations

The Coptic Orthodox Church holds the first three ecumenical councils (Nicaea 325, Constantinople 381, Ephesus 431) and rejects Chalcedon (451). For Christological readings, this means:
- Cyril of Alexandria is the supreme Christological authority (mia physis tou Theou Logou sesarkomene, "one nature of the Word of God incarnate")
- Athanasius and Cyril together form the Christological backbone
- Patriarchs of Alexandria after Cyril (Dioscorus, and the post-Chalcedonian Coptic patriarchs) carry weight in the Coptic tradition
- Severus of Antioch (c. 465 to 538) is highly regarded in the Oriental Orthodox tradition

Coptic theology is heir to the Alexandrian school and tends toward strong allegorical and typological reading. The Theotokos receives extensive Old Testament typological treatment in Coptic hymnography (the Theotokia for the days of the week).

When working with passages where Christological formulation matters (the Council of Chalcedon's "in two natures"), the skill should be aware of the difference between Eastern Orthodox and Oriental Orthodox formulations and not import language that would be foreign to a Coptic Orthodox reader.

## Direct quote gems

Patristic material should not be all summary. When a lesson or explanation uses a substantive Father, ask whether a verified sentence or tightly connected excerpt would add authenticity, beauty, and weight. If yes, include it after the summary or paraphrase.

A direct quote is useful only when it does interpretive work. Do not quote as ornament. First explain what the Father is teaching in plain language, then let the Father's own words sharpen, confirm, or deepen that point. If exact wording or source status is not verified, paraphrase instead.

## How to use this reference

When the skill needs to cite a Father:
1. If you genuinely know a Father's distinctive position on the passage, attribute it.
2. If you know the consensus but not the specific source, attribute to "the Fathers" or "the patristic tradition."
3. Summarize/paraphrase the Father's teaching first. If exact wording and source status are verified, add a brief direct quote gem for authenticity; if the quote is unverified, paraphrase instead.
4. For passages with major Christological weight, prefer Cyril of Alexandria, Athanasius, and Maximus the Confessor.
5. For passages with major moral or pastoral weight, prefer Chrysostom, Basil, and Isaac the Syrian.
6. For Old Testament typology, prefer Ephrem the Syrian, Cyril of Alexandria, and Maximus.
7. For ascetic and inner application, prefer the desert tradition, Macarius, Cassian, Climacus.
8. For Wisdom literature and the Song of Songs, prefer Gregory of Nyssa and Maximus.
9. For the Psalms, prefer Athanasius, Augustine, and Theodoret.
