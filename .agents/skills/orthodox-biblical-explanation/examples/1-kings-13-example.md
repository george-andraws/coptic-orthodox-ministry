# 1 Kings 13: The Man of God from Judah, the Old Prophet, and the Lion

## Opening Summary

A true prophet performs true miracles, names a future king, and then dies under God's judgment after disobeying the command he had faithfully repeated. This is one of the strangest chapters in the Old Testament, and one of the most piercing. The meal is not trivial because bread is dangerous; it matters because a false claim of revelation has displaced a clear command. The narrative brings prophetic gifts and personal obedience into a disturbing contrast. It also leaves a hard question: why does the deceived man die while the deceiver lives? We should not smooth that difficulty away to make an easy moral.

## Historical and Scriptural Context

The kingdom of Israel has just torn in two. Solomon is dead. Rehoboam refused to lighten the people's burden, and Israel made Jeroboam son of Nebat king (1 Kings 12). Jeroboam fears that sacrifice at Jerusalem will draw his subjects' hearts back to the house of David (12:26-27). He makes two golden calves, placing one in Bethel and the other in Dan, and tells the people: "Here are your gods, O Israel, which brought you up from the land of Egypt!" (12:28, NKJV). He establishes a feast, appoints priests who are not Levites, and offers sacrifice at Bethel. Political fear has begun to dictate worship.

Chapter 13 places a public prophetic confrontation at Jeroboam's altar. It follows the account of the invented feast and priesthood in 12:31-33 and ends by returning to that same policy in 13:33-34. The narrative holds the king's worship and the prophet's obedience under the word of the Lord; neither royal power nor prophetic office is exempt.

## Geographical Significance ⛰️

The geographical contrast supplied by the narrative is enough to establish the stakes: Jeroboam offers Bethel and Dan as alternatives to going up to Jerusalem (1 Kings 12:27-30), and the man of God comes from Judah to Bethel (13:1). We need not reconstruct every pilgrim's route to see how worship has become entangled with the kingdom's division.

Bethel also recalls Jacob's encounter in Genesis 28. Read alongside that earlier narrative, Jeroboam's sanctuary presents a bitter contrast: a place associated with God's self-disclosure now hosts worship devised to protect a king's power. This is a canonical comparison, not a claim to have located the altar on the exact ground of Jacob's dream.

The man of God comes "from Judah" (13:1). He crosses into the setting of Jeroboam's worship and speaks at its altar. The narrative supplies his origin and commission, not a travel diary or a description of his private feelings.

## The Characters 👥

**Jeroboam son of Nebat.** King of the northern kingdom in this narrative. The people make him king in 1 Kings 12:20; his fear and alternative worship follow in 12:26-33. The sequence is clear, but these chapters do not date the confrontation to his first months, and this example does not assign a precise interval.

**The man of God from Judah.** Unnamed throughout, and usually designated by his commission as a man of God. He is also called a prophet in verse 23; the old prophet addresses him as a fellow prophet in verse 18. The contrast is not between a man who is never a prophet and one who is. It is between a true commission, a deceptive claim, and the obedience required of their bearers.

**The old prophet of Bethel.** Also unnamed. His sons report the confrontation to him; he pursues the man of God and lies about an angelic message (13:11-18). The chapter does not tell us his earlier response to the calf cult or explain why he lies. Silence about his past is not evidence of a particular motive.

**Josiah.** Named as a future son of David's house in 13:2. The narrative's fulfillment is recorded in 2 Kings 23:15-20, including the destruction of Bethel's altar and the sparing of the man of God's bones. The point here is the word's fulfillment, not an independently established calculation of years before Josiah's birth.

## Light from the Word 🕯️

### Verses 1 to 3: The prophecy at the altar

> "And behold, a man of God went from Judah to Bethel by the word of the Lord, and Jeroboam stood by the altar to burn incense. Then he cried out against the altar by the word of the Lord, and said, 'O altar, altar! Thus says the Lord: "Behold, a child, Josiah by name, shall be born to the house of David; and on you he shall sacrifice the priests of the high places who burn incense on you, and men's bones shall be burned on you."' And he gave a sign the same day, saying, 'This is the sign which the Lord has spoken: Surely the altar shall split apart, and the ashes on it shall be poured out.'"

The prophet addresses the altar itself. The altar is the defendant. The doubled address, "O altar, altar," makes the focus unmistakable: the king's arrangement of worship stands under judgment. The sign will strike the very structure before which he stands.

Josiah is named before his appearance in the story. In 2 Kings 23:16 the destruction of the altar is explicitly connected to this word. The messenger will die, but the message will not die with him. That contrast is available in the biblical narrative itself; it does not need an unverified patristic endorsement.

### Verses 4 to 6: The withered hand

> "So it came to pass when King Jeroboam heard the saying of the man of God, who cried out against the altar in Bethel, that he stretched out his hand from the altar, saying, 'Arrest him!' Then his hand, which he stretched out toward him, withered, so that he could not pull it back to himself. The altar also was split apart, and the ashes poured out from the altar, according to the sign which the man of God had given by the word of the Lord."

Jeroboam stretches out his hand against the prophet, and the hand withers. The royal arm raised against the word of God is the very member that fails him. Here the narrative places the bodily sign directly after the command to seize the messenger. That particular sequence is not a license to diagnose other people's illnesses as punishments for pride.

The altar splits. The ashes pour out. The threatened messenger cannot be seized, and the sign he announced occurs. Read together, the hand and the altar expose the limits of the king's power: he cannot command his own hand, much less silence God's word.

Jeroboam begs: "Please entreat the favor of the Lord your God" (13:6, NKJV). He seeks the intercession of the man he has just threatened. The man of God prays, and the hand is restored. But the heart is not: verse 33 states that Jeroboam did not turn from his evil way. Bodily relief and repentance are not identical. The contrast rests on the chapter's ending, not on a claim to read the king's whole soul from a pronoun. His hand returns; his policy of false worship remains.

### Verses 7 to 10: The refusal and the other way

> "Then the king said to the man of God, 'Come home with me and refresh yourself, and I will give you a reward.' But the man of God said to the king, 'If you were to give me half your house, I would not go in with you; nor would I eat bread nor drink water in this place. For so it was commanded me by the word of the Lord, saying, "You shall not eat bread, nor drink water, nor return by the same way you came."' So he went another way and did not return by the way he came to Bethel."

Do not eat. Do not drink. Do not return by the same road. The command gives the messenger a concrete obedience, and verse 10 records his departure by another way. The later return matters because it reverses what he had just refused, not because roads need to be assigned a hidden symbolic meaning.

So far, this is a portrait of a faithful prophet. He refuses royal hospitality and the promised reward. He will soon meet another invitation, backed not by royal generosity but by a claim of revelation. What he resists from the king he will accept from a fellow prophet.

### Verses 11 to 19: The old prophet of Bethel

> "Now an old prophet dwelt in Bethel, and his sons came and told him all the works that the man of God had done that day in Bethel; they also told their father the words which he had spoken to the king."

He saddles his donkey, finds the man of God sitting under an oak, and invites him home. The man of God refuses, repeating the divine command. Then the old prophet claims that an angel has authorized the opposite:

> "I too am a prophet as you are, and an angel spoke to me by the word of the Lord, saying, 'Bring him back with you to your house, that he may eat bread and drink water.'" (He was lying to him.)

The text states it flatly. He lied. The man of God goes back with him. He breaks the command at a meal in the liar's house.

This is a hinge of the narrative: the command has not been forgotten, but a second claim to divine authority is allowed to displace it. The narrator removes one uncertainty for the reader by explicitly identifying the lie. That does not make the deceiver innocent, nor does the later judgment explain every question about the two men's unequal fates. As a pastoral application, the scene calls for discernment rather than credulity before religious claims. It is not evidence that a reader's first private impression must never be corrected.

The tree locates the encounter. The text does not identify his sitting there as a sin or explain why he stopped. We should not make ordinary rest the secret fault for which he dies.

### Verses 20 to 22: The true word in the liar's mouth

> "Now it happened, as they sat at the table, that the word of the Lord came to the prophet who had brought him back; and he cried out to the man of God who came from Judah, saying, 'Thus says the Lord: "Because you have disobeyed the word of the Lord, and have not kept the commandment which the Lord your God commanded you, but you came back, ate bread, and drank water in the place of which the Lord said to you, 'Eat no bread and drink no water,' your corpse shall not come to the tomb of your fathers."'"

A real word of judgment is delivered by the man who just lied. The narrator distinguishes his fabricated angelic message from the word that now comes to him. True speech at the table does not retroactively make the earlier deception true. The presence of a genuine commission cannot be used to certify every claim its bearer makes.

### Verses 23 to 25: The lion

> "So it was, after he had eaten bread and after he had drunk, that he saddled the donkey for him, the prophet whom he had brought back. When he was gone, a lion met him on the road and killed him. And his corpse was thrown on the road, and the donkey stood by it. The lion also stood by the corpse."

A lion meets him on the road and kills him. But the lion does not eat the body or tear the donkey (13:28). The donkey stands next to the lion. Travelers see the body and the lion standing beside it. The scene is terrible precisely because the killing is followed by restraint. The old prophet identifies it as the announced judgment in verse 26; the animal's presence does not make the death meaningless predation.

Augustine offers a specific reading of this restraint in *On the Care of the Dead* 9-10. He understands the death as temporal chastening rather than evidence of the man's eternal condemnation. In section 10 he says that God "of a slayer made it to be a keeper".[^augustine] That is Augustine's interpretation of this scene, not a demonstrated unanimous verdict of the Fathers. His wider argument also matters: bodies denied an honorable burial are not thereby excluded from resurrection. The preserved body here must not become an accusation against those whose bodies suffer desecration.

### Verses 26 to 32: The burial

> "Now when the prophet who had brought him back from the way heard it, he said, 'It is the man of God who was disobedient to the word of the Lord. Therefore the Lord has delivered him to the lion, which has torn him and killed him, according to the word of the Lord which He spoke to him.'"

The old prophet retrieves the body, lays it in his own tomb, and mourns, "Alas, my brother!" He instructs his sons:

> "When I am dead, then bury me in the tomb where the man of God is buried; lay my bones beside his bones. For the saying which he cried out by the word of the Lord against the altar in Bethel, and against all the shrines on the high places which are in the cities of Samaria, will surely come to pass."

He affirms the word of the man he helped lead astray. As a pastoral meditation, we might say that he confesses by his bones where the truth lies. But that phrase is an editorial inference, not proof that the text declares his repentance complete. Augustine reads the burial request in terms of concern that his own bones be spared (*On the Care of the Dead* 9). In 2 Kings 23:17-18, both men's bones are indeed left undisturbed. The request has a narrated outcome; his final spiritual state is not ours to pronounce.

### Verses 33 to 34: Jeroboam unrepentant

> "After this event Jeroboam did not turn from his evil way, but again he made priests from every class of people for the high places; whoever wished, he consecrated him, and he became one of the priests of the high places. And this thing was the sin of the house of Jeroboam, so as to exterminate and destroy it from the face of the earth."

The chapter ends not with the prophet but with the king. The reader has seen a withered hand, a restored hand, a split altar, a dead prophet, a standing lion, and a mourning brother. Jeroboam's response is stated with terrible plainness: he did not turn. We need not suppose he personally witnessed every event to feel the force of that ending. The hand is restored; the heart remains unturned. A miracle has brought relief without bringing a change of worship.

## Spiritual Fruit 🍇

**Religious language does not authenticate a claim.** The man of God repeated a definite command, then accepted a contradictory message because another prophet claimed an angel had spoken. The kindness of the offer did not establish its origin. In our discernment, a claimed revelation still needs testing within Scripture and the Church; this story does not make a first private impression infallible or forbid seeking correction and counsel.

**A gift is not a substitute for fidelity.** The man of God's word is vindicated even though he fails to keep his command. For those who teach or serve, this is a sober distinction: usefulness to others does not remove our own need for prayer, discernment, and obedience. Service is a gift to receive humbly, not a certificate that every decision we make is sound.

**The deceiver's responsibility remains.** The narrator calls the old prophet's message a lie. We should not turn the death of the deceived man into an excuse for manipulation by a religious authority. A teacher's words can harm; age, office, and spiritual vocabulary do not remove the obligation to speak truthfully.

**Healing can be received with thanksgiving and a changed life.** Jeroboam's hand was restored. His heart was not turned. We pray for bodily healing and are right to; the narrative asks us not to receive mercy while protecting the very wrong it exposes. It does not teach that an unhealed person lacks faith, that illness proves guilt, or that the suffering must earn relief through repentance.

**Leave the final judgment to God.** The old prophet mourns and affirms the coming fulfillment, but the story does not disclose everything about his heart. Augustine's reading also refuses to identify the man of God's violent death with eternal condemnation. We can take the warning seriously without pretending to know the final state of either man.

## Closing Meditation

The hand is restored, yet Jeroboam does not turn. The prophet dies, yet the word he carried is fulfilled. These are not comfortable contrasts. As an editorial prayer arising from them: Lord Jesus Christ, keep us truthful in what we teach, teachable in what we receive, and ready to turn when mercy exposes what needs to change.
