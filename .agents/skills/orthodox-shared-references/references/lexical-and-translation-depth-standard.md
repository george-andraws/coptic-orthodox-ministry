# Lexical and Translation Depth Standard for Bible Studies

Use this standard whenever a Bible-study guide includes Greek, Hebrew, Aramaic, Syriac, Septuagint, or translation-shape observations.

## Core principle

A language note is valuable only when it changes how the reader understands the passage. It should reveal movement in the text, sharpen theology, protect against a shallow or false reading, connect Scripture to worship or the Fathers, or give the servant a concrete teaching handle.

Do not treat “linguistic insight” as a generic explanation of an English phrase. A sentence like “He who sent me places John's ministry under divine command” may be true, but it is not a language insight unless the wording, original term, translation choice, or source-text shape reveals something the English reader would likely miss.

Do not add vocabulary trivia. A note like “the Greek word means love” or “the Hebrew word means see” is usually not enough. The note must answer: **What does this wording make visible that the English reader would likely miss?**

For New Testament guides, actively look for meaningful original-language insight when the passage warrants it. Greek and occasional Aramaic terms can carry theological weight that English flattens: λόγος / Logos as “Word” in John, παράκλητος / Paraklētos behind “Helper” or “Comforter” for the Holy Spirit, or distinct Greek love words such as ἀγάπη / agapē, ἀγαπάω / agapaō, φιλέω / phileō, φιλαδελφία / philadelphia, φιλοξενία / philoxenia, and disordered `phil-` compounds when English flattens them as “love,” “beloved,” “brotherly love,” “hospitality,” or “lovers of...” Include these only when they deepen interpretation, Christology, pneumatology, sacramental meaning, worship, pastoral guardrails, or spiritual application.

## What excellent lexical depth does

Good language work usually does one or more of these:

1. **Shows movement or escalation in the passage.** Example: in John 6, English “eat” hides the movement from ordinary ἐσθίω / φάγω eating language to the more concrete τρώγω after the hearers object to Christ's flesh.
2. **Distinguishes similar English renderings.** If one English word translates several Greek or Hebrew terms, explain the difference and why the author chose or shifted terms. This matters especially in NT passages where English “love,” “word,” “helper,” “comforter,” “repent,” “witness,” “believe,” or “abide” may hide a more precise Greek term, a deliberate repetition, or a theological title.
3. **Unpacks key theological terms whose English gloss is too thin.** Explain the original term when the word itself carries doctrinal or spiritual weight, such as Logos for Christ, Paraklētos for the Holy Spirit, or agapaō / phileō distinctions when the authorial choice affects the passage. Do not force a distinction where context or scholarship does not support it.
4. **Protects doctrine and worship.** Use wording details to guard against symbol-only, crude, moralistic, anti-incarnational, anti-sacramental, anti-Trinitarian, anti-Jewish, or other distorted readings when the text itself warrants it.
5. **Explains source-shape or numbering differences.** LXX / MT / NKJV / Brenton differences matter when they change verse order, emphasis, patristic interpretation, lectionary matching, or the reader's ability to follow the text.
6. **Reveals biblical intertextuality.** Show echoes, repeated roots, wordplay, wilderness or Exodus language, sacrificial vocabulary, covenant terms, or Johannine / Pauline repeated vocabulary when it affects meaning.
7. **Supports patristic and liturgical reading.** If a Father, hymn, lectionary placement, or icon depends on a word or translation shape, explain the word where it appears in the passage.
8. **Deepens spiritual application.** The note should lead somewhere: repentance, prayer, Eucharistic reverence, watchfulness, humility, hope, mercy, or communion with Christ.
9. **Names and places.** When a Hebrew or Greek name or place has theological payoff, give the sense and why it matters here: Nathanael, Cephas, Nazareth / *netzer*, Cana, Galilee, Bethsaida. Do not add onomastic trivia.

## Required handling

For each language insight:

1. **Quote or name the exact phrase in context.** Identify the verse or line.
2. **Give the original term when useful.** Use Greek/Hebrew/Aramaic script when possible, and transliteration if it helps a normal reader.
3. **Translate plainly.** Define the term in normal English without pretending the lexicon entry itself settles the whole interpretation.
4. **Explain why it matters.** Tie it to the passage's argument, Christology, pneumatology, sacramental reality, pastoral guardrail, worship, or spiritual fruit.
5. **Show the reader's gain.** State what the original wording, repeated root, term choice, or translation shape lets the reader see that a generic English explanation would miss.
6. **Add direct source URLs in source notes when online lexical/interlinear sources are used.** Do not cite vague homepages.

## Guardrails

- Do not overclaim. A lexical observation can support an interpretation, but a single word rarely proves the whole doctrine by itself.
- Do not flatten later or broader usage. If a word can mean both a vivid concrete action and ordinary use in later Greek, say so.
- Do not use language notes to show off. If the note does not help a parish servant teach the passage better, omit it.
- Do not collect all language notes into a late appendix in guide bodies. Put each one under the passage section it illuminates.
- Do not let language notes replace exegesis. The note should serve the text's movement, not become a detached mini-lecture.

## Model example: John 6 eating verbs

Strong pattern:

> John's repeated English “eat” hides a Greek progression. Through verse 53, the forms are tied to φάγω, ordinary eating or consuming language that supplies certain tenses for ἐσθίω. In verses 54, 56, 57, and 58, John uses τρώγω: to gnaw or chew, and more generally to eat. The shift should not be exaggerated into a claim that τρώγω can only mean animal-like gnawing in every context. But John places it exactly where the dispute over Christ's flesh becomes sharp. The language becomes more concrete and sustained.
>
> After the question, “How can this Man give us His flesh to eat?” Jesus does not retreat into a safer metaphor. In verse 58, the fathers “ate” (ἔφαγον) manna and died, but the one “eating / feeding on” (ὁ τρώγων) this bread lives forever. The English word “eat” can miss the contrast. John presses the reader from ordinary eating, to the manna type, to real communion with the life-giving flesh of the incarnate Word.

Why this works:

- It distinguishes multiple Greek terms hidden behind one English word.
- It explains textual movement, not isolated vocabulary.
- It strengthens Eucharistic realism while avoiding crude or exaggerated claims.
- It ties the lexical note to Incarnation, manna typology, resurrection, and communion.
- It gives a servant something concrete to teach.

Weak pattern:

> The Greek word for eat is τρώγω, which means gnaw. Therefore John 6 proves the Eucharist is literal.

Why this fails:

- It overclaims.
- It ignores broader usage.
- It makes the word do the whole theological argument alone.
- It risks crude imagination instead of spiritual, incarnational, Eucharistic reading.
