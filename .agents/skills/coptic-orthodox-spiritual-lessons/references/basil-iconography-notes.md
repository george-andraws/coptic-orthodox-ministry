# St. Basil the Great Iconography Notes

Use when revising or teaching the Basil the Great adult-meeting lesson, especially when the lesson includes Macrina, the Basileias, the Three Holy Hierarchs, or the Coptic Liturgy of St. Basil.

## St. Basil as hierarch

Common Orthodox depiction:
- Bishop/hierarch vested in phelonion/felonion and omophorion.
- One hand blessing.
- One hand holding the Gospel book, a closed book, or a scroll.
- Long, narrow face, high forehead, long dark beard, sometimes gray or auburn depending on iconographic school.
- Inscription usually identifies him as St. Basil the Great, Archbishop of Caesarea.

Teaching significance:
- Vestments point to altar and episcopal shepherding.
- Gospel/book points to teaching and the writings that shaped the Church.
- Blessing hand points to pastoral authority.
- He should not be presented as a private religious thinker, but as a bishop of the Church.

## Three Holy Hierarchs

St. Basil is often shown with St. Gregory the Theologian and St. John Chrysostom.

Teaching significance:
- Places Basil in the living teaching tradition of the Church.
- Helps avoid making him a standalone hero or isolated genius.
- Useful for Church Fathers series lessons because it visually teaches reception, continuity, and ecclesial authority.

## Vita icon: Saint Basil with scenes from his life

Useful source: Menil Collection, “Saint Basil with Scenes from His Life,” 13th century, Cyprus or Egypt/Sinai.

Menil identifies Basil through:
- long black beard
- distinctive crosses on the omophorion
- surrounding biographical scenes

The Menil note also connects him with his influential writings, the monastery/hospital he built, and the liturgy associated with his name.

Teaching significance:
- Excellent visual bridge for the Basil lesson because it holds together writing, liturgy, charity, and life-scenes.
- Use it to say: the icon does not let us reduce Basil to one dimension.

## St. Macrina the Younger

Her iconographic type is less standardized than Basil’s episcopal type. She is often shown as:
- venerable monastic woman or ascetic
- dark veil or monastic habit
- holding a cross or scroll, or in prayer/teaching posture

Teaching significance:
- Basil’s public episcopal fruit had a hidden ascetic root.
- Macrina should not be treated as background family color. She is a spiritual force behind Basil’s conversion.
- Contrast: Basil’s vestments show public shepherding; Macrina’s monastic habit shows hidden holiness and spiritual motherhood.

## Holy Family of St. Basil

Modern Orthodox icons of the Holy Family of St. Basil gather members such as:
- St. Basil the Elder
- St. Emmelia
- St. Macrina the Elder
- St. Macrina the Younger
- St. Basil the Great
- St. Gregory of Nyssa
- St. Peter of Sebaste
- sometimes Theosebia and other family figures depending on tradition

A modern feast of the Holy Family of St. Basil was established by the Holy Synod of the Church of Greece in 1998 and is commemorated on the second Sunday of January.

Teaching significance:
- Basil did not become Basil alone.
- A Christian household can become a small church where prayer, Scripture, hospitality, asceticism, correction, and love form saints over generations.

## Slide sequence for the Basil lesson

1. St. Basil as hierarch: bishop, liturgist, teacher, shepherd.
2. St. Basil with scenes from his life: whole-life holiness, not only writings.
3. Three Holy Hierarchs: Basil inside the Church’s teaching tradition.
4. St. Macrina the Younger: hidden ascetic and spiritual influence.
5. Holy Family of St. Basil: sanctity nurtured in the Christian household.

## Accuracy cautions

- Iconographic details vary by local school and period. Do not overclaim a single exact depiction.
- Basil’s hierarch type is stable; exact vestment details, beard color, book/scroll/crosier, and surrounding saints vary.
- Macrina’s iconographic type is less standardized than Basil’s.
- Use icons as visual theology, not decoration or stock images.
